# Copywriter

Write like a smart friend who works in healthcare. Not a doctor talking down. Not a tech company trying to sound important. A real person who gets it and gets to the point.

## Before Writing

1. Read `brand/voice-and-tone/brand-voice.md` — source of truth for voice, tone slider, and word choices
2. Know your audience and context — the tone slider in brand-voice.md calibrates warmth per situation

## Quick Reference

| Component | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | Max 8 words. Outcome or emotion, not feature. | "Understand your healthcare" |
| **Subheadline** | One sentence. The "so what." | "Get a clear summary after every appointment." |
| **Feature card** | Benefit headline → one sentence what → (optional) how | "Never forget what your doctor said" |
| **CTA** | Verb-first. Short. | "Download Ditto" / "Try it free" |
| **FAQ question** | How a real person would ask it | "Is my recording private?" |
| **FAQ answer** | Under 3 sentences. Link for detail. | Direct answer, then context if needed. |

## The Process

### 1. Start with the Point

Write the one thing you want the reader to take away. That's your headline. Everything else supports it. Lead with the point, not context.

### 2. Write It Human

Read it out loud. If you wouldn't say it to a friend, rewrite it.

- "Enables seamless information sharing" → "Share it with your family"
- "We leverage AI" → describe what it does, skip the word "AI"

### 3. Tighten, Don't Flatten

The goal isn't fewer words — it's zero wasted ones. A long sentence that earns its length is better than three choppy fragments.

- Remove filler: "very," "really," "actually," "basically," "simply"
- Remove repetition: if two sentences say the same thing, one goes
- Remove words that don't change meaning — but keep the ones that add texture
- If a headline and body say the same thing, cut the body

### 4. Check Rhythm and Texture

Monotony kills attention. Vary sentence length, structure, and energy. A short punch after a longer thought creates emphasis. Two long sentences in a row feels heavy. Five short ones in a row feels robotic.

Bad: All sentences are the same length and structure and they run together and nothing stands out.

Also bad: Short sentences. All of them. Like this. Gets old. Fast.

Good: Healthcare is confusing. You leave the doctor's office with a head full of information — and forget most of it by the time you get home. Ditto fixes that.

## Consumer vs. Professional

| | Consumer (patients, caregivers) | Professional (doctors, insurers) |
|---|---|---|
| **Tone** | Warm, personal, conversational | Credible, respectful, outcome-focused |
| **Language** | "Your doctor" / "your family" | "Your patients" / "clinical workflow" |
| **Proof** | User count, ratings, press | Research data, partnerships, outcomes |
| **Pain point** | "You forget. Your family doesn't know." | "Patients forget. Conversations repeat." |
| **CTA** | "Download Ditto" | "Partner with us" / "Let's talk" |

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Writing for a committee instead of a person | Use "you." Read it aloud. Would you say this? |
| Burying the point under context | First sentence = the point. Always. |
| Using "Learn more" as a CTA | Learn more about what? Be specific: "See how it works" |
| Saying "We take X seriously" | Show, don't tell. Describe the architecture, not the sentiment. |
| Same sentence length throughout | Vary it. Short punch. Longer breath. Short again. |
| Feature-first instead of benefit-first | "Record your appointment" → "Never forget what your doctor said" |
| Writing "patients" or "users" in consumer copy | It's "you." Always "you." |

## Quality Check

Before any copy is final:

- [ ] Lands on the first read? (Clear ≠ simple. Clear = zero confusion.)
- [ ] Does every sentence do something the previous one didn't?
- [ ] Tighter than your first draft?
- [ ] Sounds like it was written, not assembled?
- [ ] Rhythm varies — not all short, not all long?
- [ ] One clear next step for the reader?
- [ ] No jargon, no filler, no corporate speak?
