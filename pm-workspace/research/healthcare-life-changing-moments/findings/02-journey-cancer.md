# The Cancer / Oncology Patient Journey — Lifecycle Pain Points (Patient + Loved Ones)

*Research file for ditto.care patient-experience research. Geography: pan-EU, anchored on Netherlands (IKNL/KWF) and DACH (Deutsche Krebshilfe / DKFZ-Krebsinformationsdienst). Compiled 2026-05-31.*

**Source tiers:** Tier 1 = peer-reviewed / meta-analyses / EU & national bodies · Tier 2 = named cancer patient orgs & clinical guidelines · Tier 3 = forums / news / vendor blogs (texture only, flagged). **US-derived statistics are flagged "(US data — EU applicability uncertain)".** Each finding carries a Confidence rating (High / Medium / Low).

---

## Key takeaways

1. **Comprehension and recall failure is the structural bottleneck of the whole journey.** Patients forget 40–80% of medical information immediately after a consultation, and roughly half of what they *do* retain is recalled incorrectly — and this gets worse the more information is given and the more anxious the patient is ([Kessels 2003, Tier 1](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014107680309600504)). Cancer consultations are exactly the high-volume, high-anxiety setting where this fails hardest. **Confidence: High.**

2. **Patients are made the de-facto coordinators of their own fragmented care.** In the All.Can pan-European survey (~4,000 patients, 10+ countries), diagnosis was the single most-cited area of inefficiency (26%), and information-sharing / coordination across specialists was a top improvement area ([All.Can / European Cancer Organisation, Tier 2](https://www.europeancancer.org/resources/news/news-patient-insights-on-cancer-care-opportunities-for-improving-efficiency.html)). No one entity holds the overview; the patient and family do. **Confidence: High.**

3. **A large fraction of patients fundamentally misunderstand their own prognosis and treatment intent.** 69% of metastatic lung-cancer and 81% of metastatic colorectal-cancer patients did not understand that chemotherapy was *not* likely to cure them ([Weeks et al., NEJM 2012, Tier 1 — US data, EU applicability uncertain](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1204410)). Internationally, about half of advanced-cancer patients are unaware of their prognosis ([prognostic communication review, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7181418/)). **Confidence: High (effect), Medium (EU magnitude).**

4. **Loved ones carry a measurable, under-recognized clinical burden.** European caregivers show significantly higher depression, anxiety and insomnia odds and far more lost work than non-caregivers ([Goren et al. 2014, Tier 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24496758/)). Their single biggest unmet need is *information* — about side-effect management at home and treatment risks/benefits — yet they often have no formal information access. **Confidence: High.**

5. **Financial toxicity exists even in strong-welfare EU systems and is driven by income loss, not co-pays.** Across 25 European countries, cancer patients reported income loss, out-of-pocket costs and even care avoidance for cost reasons ([Bhanna et al., ESMO Open 2025 / NKI, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12180988/)). In Germany 81% reported out-of-pocket costs and 37% income loss; financial toxicity there is driven by lost income rather than co-payments ([BMC Cancer 2020, Tier 1](https://bmccancer.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12885-020-07028-4)). **Confidence: High.**

6. **Diagnostic limbo is a distinct, high-distress phase that the system treats as "just waiting."** Anxiety and depression peak during the suspected-cancer / waiting interval, driven by uncertainty rather than the eventual diagnosis ([HRQoL diagnostic-phase study, Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4873991/)). Dutch diagnostic intervals range from ~1 week (breast/melanoma) to ~8 weeks (prostate), with a long tail for 10–25% of patients ([Helsper et al., EJC 2017, Tier 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959804917313436)). **Confidence: High.**

7. **Supportive/palliative care arrives too late.** ~75% of European lung-cancer specialists refer to palliative care only when treatment options are exhausted or at the very end of life; only ~22% refer earlier ([Tier 1 survey of EU specialists](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4279692/)). Early integration is an EU policy standard but not the lived reality ([European Cancer Organisation Code, Tier 2](https://www.europeancancer.org/2-standard/88-the-code-integrated-palliative-and-supportive-care)). **Confidence: High.**

8. **The most overlooked problems are the normalized ones:** the forgotten consultation, the loved one with no information rights, the patient acting as their own care coordinator, and the un-checked assumption that the patient understood. These are invisible because they're expected.

## Stage 1 — Pre-diagnosis / diagnostic limbo

**The waiting itself is the harm.** Health-related quality of life, anxiety and depression are at their worst during the diagnostic phase of suspected cancer — and the distress is driven by *uncertainty*, not by the disease severity that is later confirmed ([HRQoL in the diagnostic phase, Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4873991/)). **Confidence: High.**

- **Diagnostic intervals are long and uneven.** In the Netherlands, median diagnostic interval ranges from ~1 week (breast, melanoma) to ~8 weeks (prostate); a substantial tail of 10–25% of patients wait far longer ([Helsper et al., EJC 2017, Tier 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959804917313436)). Symptomatic gastric/oesophageal cancers in NL show clear "room for improvement" in time-to-diagnosis ([Maastricht/PMC, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7268938/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Patients are left to interpret ambiguous signals alone.** Once a clinician voices concern about possible cancer, fear and stress begin and persist through every subsequent test; patients report the *not knowing* as the worst part ([Dutch patient-journey synthesis, Tier 3 — texture](https://sakura.eu/tdx-knowledge-hub/patient-journey-research-netherlands)). **Confidence: Medium.**
- **Overlooked overview gap:** during limbo there is no owner of the open question ("when will I hear, from whom, what does this test mean?"). The patient holds the only running timeline. **Confidence: Medium.**

## Stage 2 — Diagnosis delivery

This is the moment where recall failure does the most damage, because the most consequential information is delivered in the highest-anxiety state.

- **The forgetting curve.** Patients forget **40–80%** of information immediately, and **~half of what is retained is recalled incorrectly**; recall *worsens* as the amount of information increases and as anxiety rises ([Kessels 2003, JRSM, Tier 1](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014107680309600504); summarized [PMC mirror](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC544650/)). This is the single most-cited, most-normalized failure in the journey. **Confidence: High.**
- **Written summaries / recordings recover a meaningful slice.** Providing written consultation summaries improves recall (one estimate: ~+20% of information correctly recalled) ([Tier 3 vendor summary of the literature, flagged](https://www.dyaclinical.com/blog/patient-recall-consultation-summary)). The underlying point — that recall is recoverable with simple artifacts — is well established. **Confidence: Medium.**
- **Information volume vs. comprehension.** Across the cancer journey, patients report being given either too little or too much, unstructured information; in a large European patient/caregiver survey reported via lung-cancer SDM work, ~40% said they did not have enough information and ~29% did not understand the information they found ([Healio reporting of a 34-country WHO-region survey, Tier 3 — single secondary source, flagged](https://www.healio.com/news/hematology-oncology/20250917/communication-knowledge-critical-to-improve-shared-decisionmaking-for-lung-cancer)). **Confidence: Low (single secondary source; treat figures as indicative).**
- **Loved-one involvement at the worst moment.** The "second pair of ears" is informally relied upon but not systematized — whether a family member is present, and what they're allowed to know, is ad hoc.

## Stage 3 — Treatment decision-making

- **Shared decision-making (SDM) is patchy and prognosis is under-communicated.** Oncologists are often reluctant to discuss prognosis, worried about damaging hope or the relationship; clinicians are frequently untrained in the specialty skills (breaking bad news, discussing prognosis/risk, eliciting values) that SDM requires ([prognostic disclosure scoping review, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9119949/); [Delivering High-Quality Cancer Care / NCBI, Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK202146/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Patients misunderstand the *intent* of treatment.** 69% (lung) and 81% (colorectal) of metastatic patients did not understand chemo was not curative ([Weeks et al., NEJM 2012, Tier 1 — US data, EU applicability uncertain](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1204410)). Perceiving palliative treatment as curative correlates with worse prognostic understanding ([JCO-related study, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6931912/)). **Confidence: High (effect); Medium (EU magnitude).**
- **Decision regret tracks low involvement and side effects.** Roughly **1 in 5** patients regret their treatment decision; regret is higher among those *less involved* in the decision and those who experience side effects, and persists for years (prominently studied in prostate cancer) ([decision-regret systematic review/meta-analysis, Tier 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2588931123000378); UK PROM study [Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7317932/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **"What are my options?" is hard to hold in your head.** Choices between options of similar efficacy but different side-effect profiles (e.g. prostate, early breast) are values-based and information-heavy — exactly where recall failure (Stage 2) compounds into regret (here). **Confidence: High.**
- **Tool that works:** question prompt lists (QPLs) given before consultations increase prognostic and end-of-life questions and improve satisfaction; one trial showed patients asked more prognostic questions and oncologists gave more prognostic information — though QPLs can lengthen consultations and transiently raise anxiety ([RCT, Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2375236/); [French ACP QPL RCT, Tier 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32739563/)). **Confidence: High.**

## Stage 4 — Active treatment (chemo / radiation / surgery)

- **Side-effect self-management at home is offloaded to patients and families with thin support.** Caregivers' top unmet needs are precisely *information about managing side effects at home (~78%)* and *risks/benefits of chemotherapy (~79%)* ([Informal Caregiving for Cancer Patients review, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3816360/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Logistical and administrative load is heavy and normalized.** Patients navigate complex pathways with appointments and scheduling across multiple specialty services and locations; obtaining, organizing and tracking this is resource-intensive even for clinical staff ([oncology navigation literature, Tier 3 — texture](https://honcology.com/blog/patient-navigators-enhancing-oncology-care-coordination)). Where a nurse navigator exists they become the single point of contact — but most patients in most EU systems do not have one. **Confidence: Medium.**
- **Keeping the overview during treatment:** medication schedules, blood-count thresholds, "when do I call someone," appointment sequencing across chemo/radiation/surgery — the integrating record lives in the patient's notebook or the caregiver's head, not in a shared system. **Confidence: Medium.**

## Stage 5 — Coordination across specialists (oncologist / GP / radiology / pharmacy)

- **Fragmentation and broken hand-offs are systemic.** A qualitative study of cancer-care coordination identified barriers including unclear professional roles/responsibilities, inadequate communication between specialists and primary care, and patients "falling through the cracks" during transitions of care ([Walsh et al., qualitative study, Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2891740/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **GP–oncologist communication is a known weak link.** Poor information transfer between the GP (who often holds the longitudinal relationship and comorbidities) and the oncologist degrades continuity; interventions like shared video consultations are being trialed precisely because the default is disconnected ([GP–oncologist communication review, Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7268533/); [shared video consultation RCT protocol, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6593592/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **No one holds the overview — so the patient does.** All.Can patients flagged information-sharing and "making integrated multidisciplinary care a reality" as core improvement areas; diagnosis (the first hand-off-heavy phase) was the most-cited inefficiency (26%) ([All.Can / ECO, Tier 2](https://www.europeancancer.org/resources/news/news-patient-insights-on-cancer-care-opportunities-for-improving-efficiency.html)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Pharmacy / medication reconciliation** across oral oncolytics, supportive meds and pre-existing prescriptions is a coordination surface rarely owned by any single actor. **Confidence: Medium (inferred from fragmentation literature).**

## Stage 6 — Survivorship / follow-up

- **Fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) is one of the most common and most cited unmet needs** of survivors, and unmet needs *predict* FCR severity ([FCR + unmet needs study, Tier 1/2](https://www.dovepress.com/the-impact-of-unmet-needs-on-fear-of-cancer-recurrence-in-cancer-survi-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-CMAR); [FCR systematic review, Tier 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23475398/)). Dutch survivorship data combine the NKR registry with the PROFILES patient-reported-outcomes registry to map life with and after cancer ([IKNL "Kankerzorg in beeld: over leven met en na kanker" 2019, Tier 1/2](https://iknl.nl/getmedia/fbcc6960-6755-4c36-b6c0-79d0265b18ce/kankerzorg-in-beeld-over-leven-met-en-na-kanker-(2019).pdf)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Psychological distress persists for years.** Elevated risk of sustained distress, anxiety and depression exists many years after diagnosis/treatment; distress screening (e.g. the Distress Thermometer) is built into certified German cancer centers but uptake of actual psycho-oncological support lags ([DKFZ Krebsinformationsdienst / S3 Psychoonkologie, Tier 2](https://www.krebsinformationsdienst.de/krebs-und-psyche/psychoonkologie)). **Confidence: High.**
- **The "now what?" cliff.** The transition out of active treatment removes the scaffolding of frequent contact; survivorship-care coordination is widely described as fragmented, and the patient again becomes the integrator of follow-up scheduling, late-effect monitoring and re-entry to the GP. **Confidence: Medium.**

## Stage 7 — Recurrence / palliative / end-of-life

- **Palliative care is referred too late.** ~75% of European lung-cancer specialists would refer only when no treatment options remain or at end of life; only ~22% refer earlier ([EU specialist survey, Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4279692/)). Barriers include patient misconceptions that palliative care = imminent death, and clinician fear that referral signals "abandonment" ([same; communication-intervention review, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9207918/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Prognostic awareness is low at exactly the time it matters most.** ~half of advanced-cancer patients are unaware of their prognosis; better prognostic communication is associated with more active participation in decisions and better end-of-life preparation ([prognostic-communication systematic review, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7181418/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **End-of-life care setting transitions are frequent and burdensome.** A nationwide monitoring study across four European countries documented multiple care-setting transitions in cancer patients' last three months of life ([EURO IMPACT, Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4301937/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **The "family meeting" problem.** Aligning patient, multiple loved ones and the clinical team on goals of care is rarely a structured, documented event; understanding and wishes drift apart across the people who will make decisions. **Confidence: Medium.**

## Cross-cutting: Loved ones / caregivers (all stages)

- **Quantified clinical burden.** European caregivers (n=1,713; FR/DE/IT/ES/UK) had significantly higher absenteeism (8.4% vs 4.8%), overall work impairment (26.4% vs 18.1%) and activity impairment, plus higher odds of depression (OR≈1.46), anxiety (OR≈1.97) and insomnia (OR≈1.95), and more healthcare visits/ER visits/hospitalizations than non-caregivers ([Goren et al. 2014, Supportive Care in Cancer, Tier 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24496758/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Information is the top unmet caregiver need**, and ≥33% of caregivers still need help coping with their own concerns *two years* after diagnosis ([Informal Caregiving review, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3816360/); head-and-neck caregiver cohort [Tier 1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10101897/)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Proxy access is a real, under-solved barrier.** Caregivers frequently cannot access the patient's portal/records without cumbersome, ad-hoc proxy setup; by default information is shared only on request, creating delays that affect care and cost. Privacy/legal navigation is hard for both clinicians and families ([proxy-access ethics review, Tier 1 — note US legal framing, EU/GDPR specifics differ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35437282/); [serious-illness portal use, Tier 1 — US data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9022132/)). **Confidence: Medium (problem real EU-wide; specific mechanisms US-centric).**

## Cross-cutting: Administrative & financial toxicity (all stages)

- **Income loss > co-pays in strong-welfare systems.** Germany: 81% out-of-pocket costs, 37% income loss; 24% of those with income loss lost >€1,200/month; financial toxicity driven by lost income, not co-payments ([BMC Cancer 2020, Tier 1](https://bmccancer.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12885-020-07028-4)). **Confidence: High.**
- **Pan-European pattern.** Survey of >2,500 patients across 25 European countries (NKI-led): income loss, out-of-pocket costs and care avoidance for cost reasons; higher relative financial loss associated with worse QoL and more distress; strong-welfare systems (NL, Sweden) protect better but do not eliminate the problem ([ESMO Open 2025 / NKI press, Tier 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12180988/); [NKI news, Tier 2](https://www.nki.nl/news-events/news/cancer-patients-across-europe-face-serious-financial-and-social-challenges-new-study-finds/)). **Confidence: High.**

## Most overlooked problems (ranked)

1. **The forgotten consultation — comprehension/recall is never verified.** 40–80% forgotten, ~half of the rest wrong, worse with more info and more anxiety — yet there's no routine artifact (summary/recording/teach-back) ensuring the patient and family actually retained the plan. Everything downstream (adherence, decisions, coordination) inherits this failure. [Kessels 2003](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014107680309600504). **Confidence: High.**
2. **The patient is the unpaid care coordinator.** Fragmentation across oncologist/GP/radiology/pharmacy means no one holds the overview, so the patient/family does — under maximum cognitive and emotional load. Normalized as "being an engaged patient." [Walsh, care-coordination barriers](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2891740/); [All.Can](https://www.europeancancer.org/resources/news/news-patient-insights-on-cancer-care-opportunities-for-improving-efficiency.html). **Confidence: High.**
3. **The loved one with no information rights.** Caregivers carry measurable clinical morbidity and have *information* as their #1 unmet need, yet proxy access to records/portals is ad-hoc and often blocked — so the person doing the home care can't see the data they need. [Goren 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24496758/); [proxy-access review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35437282/). **Confidence: High (burden) / Medium (access mechanism EU-specifics).**
4. **The unverified assumption of understanding — especially about prognosis/intent.** Most metastatic patients believed non-curative chemo could cure them; ~half of advanced patients don't know their prognosis. The gap is normalized because no one closes the loop. [Weeks NEJM 2012 (US)](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1204410); [prognostic-communication review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7181418/). **Confidence: High (effect); Medium (EU magnitude).**
5. **Diagnostic limbo as an untreated distress phase.** Anxiety/depression peak while *waiting*, driven by uncertainty, but the system books the patient and goes quiet — no owner of the open question, no expectation-setting, no support. [HRQoL diagnostic phase](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4873991/); [Helsper EJC 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959804917313436). **Confidence: High.**

*(Runners-up: late palliative referral driven by the "abandonment" framing; survivorship "now-what" cliff with persistent FCR; financial toxicity via income loss even in NL/DE.)*

## Low-confidence and US-derived claims (caveats for ditto.care)

- **Weeks et al. NEJM 2012 (69%/81% non-curative misunderstanding)** is **US data (CanCORS cohort)** — EU applicability uncertain, though the *direction* is corroborated by international prognostic-awareness reviews. Treat magnitude as indicative for EU.
- **"~40% lacked enough information / ~29% didn't understand it" pan-European figures** come from a **single secondary source** ([Healio reporting](https://www.healio.com/news/hematology-oncology/20250917/communication-knowledge-critical-to-improve-shared-decisionmaking-for-lung-cancer)) describing a 34-country survey I could not independently verify against the primary publication. **Confidence: Low** — do not quote as hard fact without locating the primary source.
- **Written-summary "+~20% recall improvement"** is drawn from a **Tier 3 vendor blog** summarizing the literature; the qualitative effect is well established but the exact percentage should be re-sourced to a primary trial.
- **Proxy-access specifics** are largely **US patient-portal / HIPAA-framed**; the *problem* is real EU-wide but the legal mechanisms differ under GDPR and national law — verify NL/DACH-specific rules before product claims.
- **Caregiver side-effect/risk-benefit unmet-need percentages (78%/79%)** come from a review synthesizing mixed (incl. US) European literature; directionally robust, magnitude approximate.
- Several primary sources (PMC, ESMO Open, SAGE, ECO) returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch; statistics above were captured via search-engine extraction of those same pages plus PubMed/abstract corroboration. Figures are consistent across sources but should be spot-checked against the primary PDFs before external use.
