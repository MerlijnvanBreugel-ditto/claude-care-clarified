# Low-Frequency Retention: Precedents and Patterns for Ditto Care

**Research question:** Is once-every-1-3-months use an insurmountable product problem, or are there successful precedents?

**Short answer:** It is not insurmountable, but it requires a fundamentally different retention strategy than habit-loop thinking. The products that survive infrequent use either (a) earn the right to a predictive notification that delivers real value at the exact right moment, (b) latch onto a higher-frequency adjacent behavior, or (c) become so high-value at the moment of need that users never uninstall. All three have healthcare equivalents.

---

## The Framework That Names the Problem

Reforge's ICED Theory draws a hard line: products used less than monthly sit in what they call the **Forgettable Zone**. The user's brain does not form a habit loop. The standard retention playbook (push notifications, streaks, email sequences) performs poorly here because there is no recurring stimulus to anchor the habit to.

The framework offers three legitimate escapes:

1. **Expand the use case** to a higher-frequency adjacent behavior (Calm added sleep stories; a meditation app used three times a week beats one used once a month)
2. **Latch onto a habit product** (airlines partnered with credit cards — the miles accumulate daily, the flight happens twice a year)
3. **Nail the timing** — if you cannot increase frequency, control the moment of re-engagement so precisely that the app appears before the user needs to remember it

Reforge's benchmark: products with natural frequency above once per month sit in the Habit Zone. Below that, you are engineering for a different kind of retention entirely.

## Five Products That Solved This

**TurboTax — annual use, 77% year-over-year retention**

The gold standard for episodic retention. TurboTax achieves 77% annual retention (Lenny Rachitsky's benchmark: "GOOD" for consumer transactional is 30%, "GREAT" is 50% — TurboTax is exceptional). Their mechanism: they own the outcome, not the app. Users return because switching has a cost (prior-year data, familiarity with the flow) and because the need is non-negotiable. Between tax seasons, Intuit cross-sells into Mint and a tax estimator tool — a latching strategy to maintain wallet share and top-of-mind. The lesson: if your product is the best available solution for an unavoidable life event, you do not need daily engagement to retain.

**Hopper — travel booking between trips**

Hopper solved the between-booking gap with a mechanism they call "Watch." Users actively opt in to price monitoring for a future trip they intend to take. The app then has permission to send a high-signal notification when prices hit the predicted low. Result: 65-70% push notification opt-in rate (industry average is under 50%), repeat booking rates that fuel compounding growth. The key insight: the notification earns trust because it is tied to a specific, user-declared intention. Users do not feel interrupted — they feel served. Hopper also learned that forcing notification permission at install backfires. Trust is built through demonstrated value first, permission second.

**Calm — from occasional to nightly**

Calm's retention inflected when it added Sleep Stories alongside meditation. Meditation is episodic; sleep is nightly. Adding a use case in the Habit Zone transformed retention without changing the core product. Paid subscribers who used both Sleep Stories and meditation sessions reported using the app 3-4 times per week. The mechanism was not a feature gimmick — sleep is genuinely adjacent to the anxious-mind audience Calm already owned. The lesson: a frequency expansion works when the new use case serves the same underlying need as the core product.

**OpenTable — dining reservations (median user: twice per year)**

Andrew Chen cites OpenTable as the paradigmatic low-frequency, high-value product. Median user books approximately twice per year, yet OpenTable sustains the business. Their retention mechanism is a combination of: (1) owning the booking moment so completely that alternatives feel high-friction, (2) a loyalty program (Gold status for 6+ reservations in 12 months) that gives frequent users a reason to consolidate bookings, and (3) re-engagement campaigns targeting users who have not returned in 90 days with personalized offers. OpenTable is also an example of where top-of-mind is sustained by the product category itself (dining out is a salient desire) rather than by the app's internal mechanics.

**Wysa / Ginger — mental health between clinical sessions**

These digital mental health apps face the same episodic problem as Ditto: the primary event (a therapy or coaching session) happens infrequently. Their retention mechanism: make the app useful between sessions, not just during them. Ginger's coaches continue support between clinical sessions; Wysa's adjunct in-app features (journaling, brief CBT exercises, chatbot check-ins) drove a 3x improvement in session completion rates among users who engaged with between-session features. Clinically, users who accessed at least one between-session feature were significantly more likely to return for their next scheduled appointment. Applied: the between-appointment period is not dead time to survive — it is the retention surface.

## What Actually Works for Re-engagement (4-8 Week Gap)

**Push notifications: useful but fragile**

Andrew Chen's analysis: notification-driven retention creates a "collapse model" where diminishing click-through rates erode the user base. Notifications work when they carry genuine signal (price dropped, appointment tomorrow, your doctor sent a follow-up note) and fail when they are attention-grabbing without substance. Healthcare apps using a Love Dialog — a proactive, in-app feedback request — showed 90-day retention jump from 34% to 66%. The mechanism is not the notification itself but the sense that the product is listening.

**Contextual timing, not calendar timing**

Google Maps's model: it reads your calendar event, calculates travel time, and notifies you when to leave. The notification arrives without being scheduled by the user and carries precise utility. This is the "notification utility" pattern: the app earns notification permission by using it in a way that the user could not have arranged themselves. In healthcare, the healthcare-calendar equivalent is: Ditto detects an upcoming appointment (via calendar integration or previous appointment data), surfaces pre-visit preparation at the right moment, and follows up in the 24-48 hours post-appointment when the patient is most likely to act on their summary.

**What does not work**

Generic re-engagement push at arbitrary intervals. Streak mechanics for non-habitual behaviors. Email sequences that do not reference a specific user action or upcoming event. App-store re-download campaigns (expensive, low LTV on re-acquired users). The research shows that reaching out too soon after inactivity feels intrusive; too late and the user has mentally churned.

## Key Patterns

| Pattern | Mechanism | Ditto Equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Own the unavoidable event | TurboTax — taxes happen, switching is costly | Medical appointments happen; Ditto is the obvious tool |
| Latching | Airlines + credit cards; Calm + sleep | Calendar integration; Care Circle as daily social layer |
| Declared-intent notifications | Hopper Watch — user says "I'm planning a trip" | User adds next appointment; Ditto arms pre-appointment prep |
| Between-session utility | Wysa/Ginger — value between clinical touchpoints | Symptom tracking, medication reminders, care circle updates |
| Contextual timing | Google Maps + calendar = "leave now" | Calendar pull → "your oncology appointment is in 3 days, here's your prep" |

## Implications for Ditto Care

**The base case is not as bad as it looks.** "GOOD" for consumer transactional apps (Airbnb, TurboTax, Lyft) is 30% annual retention; "GREAT" is 50%. Ditto is not being held to a daily-habit-app standard. The right benchmark cohort is appointment-driven utilities, not social apps.

**The calendar integration is the highest-leverage retention mechanism available.** It converts Ditto from a passive utility the user must remember into an active presence that appears before every appointment. This is the healthcare equivalent of Hopper Watch: user-declared intention (an upcoming appointment) unlocks the right to a high-signal, high-value notification.

**Care Circle is the latching strategy.** If a patient's loved ones check the Care Circle feed regularly, the patient has a daily ambient reminder that Ditto exists and is used. Circle members become a retention mechanism for the patient — a structure analogous to the airline/credit card partnership, but using human relationships instead of financial incentives.

**Between-appointment utility is the expansion move.** Symptom tracking between appointments, medication reminders, and preparation for the next visit extend the use case from 4 annual touchpoints to something closer to weekly. Wysa's data shows this dramatically improves return-to-next-session rates. The mechanism must be genuinely useful (not forced gamification) and tied to the upcoming appointment context to feel coherent.

**Notifications need a trust-first earn model.** Hopper's lesson applies directly: do not request notification permission at install. Build value first (the post-appointment summary is the value), then ask for permission in the context of a specific high-value future event (your next appointment). Earned notification permission in healthcare is a durable retention asset.
