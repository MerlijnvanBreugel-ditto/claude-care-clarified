01 · How we started

Medical scribes gained traction because the technology finally caught up. But the real problem was never the doctor's note — it was the patient, still working from a notepad at best, a failing memory at worst. EHRs have let patients down: clunky software, enormous fragmentation, and Latin-laced letters that disclose you have cancer.

From the start we've held a contrarian belief: the patient is massively underserved — and the patient is the unlock for a stuck healthcare system.
Focus today · severe disease — oncology primary, neurodegenerative secondary
02 · Summaries cannot be the goal

Summary tools are a commodity now.

Why the wedge won't carry us

  • Better summaries are only defensible with something no one else has — proprietary models, unique validation data, a sharp niche. We don't truly have that yet.
  • EHRs and scribes are catching up; within ~1 year, patient summaries are native. When ChatGPT or Gemini ship conversation summaries, that directly attacks us. "Doctors will never allow it" is stretching reality.
  • A summary matters at the appointment and maybe the day after — weekly at most, even when severely ill. Without delivering incredible value in that moment, we're a convenience, not something people grow attached to.
  • Low frequency is hard to monetise: consumers rarely pay for health apps; insurers need activity to justify reimbursement.
  • Pushing frequency or growth features for their own sake is a dead end. Unless we solve real problems, we can only nudge so far in the direction we want.

The good part

  • Our summaries are genuinely better, and people trust them. This is key (and we must measure it).
  • We are roughly 10× better than the alternative: forgetting.
  • Summaries and recordings hold far more potential than we unlock today.
  • The summary is the way in. We have to maximise the value we bring in that episodic moment.
  • From there: auto-update your health profile · nudge and help with follow-up tasks · inform family dynamically · link to peers · explain treatment in podcast/visual form · find a second opinion · find a fitting trial… and the list goes on.
03 · The undecided vision

Five versions of a future Ditto.

Not exclusive end-states. They can be combined — possibly layers, stacked together.

01

Care Companion

Supports you along every step of your care journey.
Understanding — making sense of what's happening, knowing what to expect.
+ disease-specific, beats general AI, holistic
− EHRs improving, needs user commitment
02

Social Care Platform

The place for shared understanding among loved ones and peers.
Connection — emotional support, not being alone.
+ network effects, the go-to health-sharing place
− competes with social media / WhatsApp
03

Care Coordination

Families manage the disease and arrange practical support.
Coordination — tasks, keeping the core care group aligned.
+ rising need, care too complex for WhatsApp
− pros need onboarding (Caren), needs whole group
04

Patient-Owned Record

You own and understand all your healthcare data.
Data — ingest everything, make it understandable and shareable.
+ EU go-to with huge lock-in if superior
− data alone is low value, gov competition, integration-dependent
05

Trusted Health Assistant

Prevents illness or gets you recovered as well as possible.
Agency — advice and nudges on the medical behaviour you control.
+ highest tech IP, strong lock-in once trusted
− competes with big tech, instant MDR / high-risk

05 · How they relate

One product, or layers?

  • These are not excluding end-states. They can be combined and merged — potentially layers stacked together.
  • But doing so risks a confused, split focus and a muddy market position.
  • 1, 3 and 5 link directly to our contrarian take: the patient is the key to better healthcare. 4 needs real intelligence to change anything. 2 improves experience and perception — maybe not the outcome.
  • I propose we explore the social vision further — I believe its upside and impact are biggest, and it opens monetisation routes, expected and unexpected.
  • Short-to-mid term: oncology favours 1, 2, 5; neurodegenerative favours 3.
  • 4 is not a goal in itself — more likely a requirement to truly nail 1 and 5.

06 · Why this matters

Every day we decide what to build and how to grow Ditto. Directionality matters — without it we ship a fragmented, unclear product and proposition. Features we ship may be discontinued; that is fine, but until then each one costs maintenance and a less-clear experience.

Bug fixes and polish will always take a share of our capacity. But in the end we should focus on what differentiates us and delivers the most value for users. If we cannot sharply articulate who our users are, which problems we solve, and why we do it better than anyone — we lose focus and efficiency.

07 · Why this might not matter

The honest counter-argument.

  • Many successful companies never articulated the mission they'd hold ten years later — but at their core there was a north star that underpinned the category.
  • The field moves so fast that we have to stay adaptable.
  • The real question: to what extent do commitments now exclude other options or make success there less likely? Going all-in on data integrations and anticipating EHDS, for example, means slowing down on adding real health intelligence to whatever data users already put into Ditto.
